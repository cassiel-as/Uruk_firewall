[ PHASE 03 — EIGHT-LAW INPUT MATRIX ]

  ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────
  LAW 3 · PHYSICS · COST
  ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────
  Traffic behaves as a compressible fluid subject to
  demand pressure. At any given moment, a proportion
  of the population has a latent desire to make a trip
  that is currently suppressed by congestion cost —
  the trip is not worth making because the road is too
  slow. When new capacity is added, the generalised
  cost of travel falls. Suppressed trips are activated.
  New travellers enter the network. New routes become
  viable. Land use shifts outward (sprawl), generating
  further trips. The new capacity is consumed.
  This is not a planning failure. It is fluid dynamics
  applied to a demand-elastic system. Adding capacity
  to a demand-elastic network without managing demand
  is thermodynamically equivalent to enlarging a
  container that generates its own contents.

  ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────
  LAW 7 · GEOGRAPHY · ANCHOR
  ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────
  Road expansion does not only fail to reduce congestion —
  it actively restructures land use in ways that make
  non-car mobility permanently less viable. Each lane
  added to an urban corridor increases the physical
  distance that must be traversed by pedestrians,
  cyclists, and public transit users. Sprawl generated
  by highway expansion increases average trip distances,
  making every mode except the private car less
  competitive. The geographic lock-in is structural:
  once land use organises around highway access, the
  political economy of transit investment collapses
  because the population density required to support
  it has been physically dispersed. Seoul's removal of
  the Cheonggyecheon elevated highway demonstrates the
  inverse: reducing road capacity triggered land use
  densification and transit uptake. The geography was
  changed by removing infrastructure, not adding it.

  ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────
  LAW 1 · Art:
  "Congestion = not enough road" is the dominant
  spatial metaphor. It frames the problem as a
  container too small for its contents — solution:
  bigger container. The metaphor forecloses
  demand management before analysis begins.

  LAW 2 · Psychology:
  Road construction is politically visible, ribbon-
  cuttable, and attributable to individual politicians.
  Demand management (pricing, restriction) is invisible
  and politically toxic. The cognitive asymmetry
  between visible supply solutions and invisible
  demand solutions sustains the assumption regardless
  of empirical evidence.

  LAW 4 · Chemistry:
  Each lane-mile of urban highway generates ~55,000
  tonnes of CO₂ over its lifetime (construction +
  induced vehicle use). The induced demand effect
  means emissions are higher than the road's
  design capacity would suggest — the road generates
  the traffic that generates the emissions.
  This transformation cost sits outside the
  infrastructure cost-benefit framework.

  LAW 5 · Science:
  Induced demand has been empirically confirmed in
  peer-reviewed literature since the 1990s. Duranton
  & Turner (2011) is the definitive meta-analysis.
  The empirical data is not uncertain — it is
  ignored at the policy layer. This is unfunded
  certainty in reverse: the data exists and is
  published, but the policy framework was not
  built to receive it.

  LAW 6 · Philosophy:
  The assumption rests on a supply-side epistemology
  of infrastructure: problems are caused by
  insufficient supply, solutions are supply additions.
  This paradigm was appropriate when infrastructure
  genuinely did not exist. Applied to mature urban
  networks with latent demand, it becomes
  self-defeating — but the paradigm has no internal
  mechanism for detecting demand elasticity.

  LAW 8 · Religion:
  The private car became a cultural symbol of freedom
  and individual sovereignty in the 20th century —
  particularly in the United States. This encoding
  makes demand management (congestion pricing,
  car restriction) culturally equivalent to
  restricting freedom, activating identity-defence
  responses that are independent of the physics
  of traffic flow.
